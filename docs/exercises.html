Exercises

Instructions

Over the course of the semester, you are required to submit written solutions to two of the following exercises. You should prepare your solution in a Quarto file (i.e., a .qmd) that contains all code needed to reproduce your analysis. Your solution should be structured similarly to the lecture notes, with code tightly integrated with exposition.

Once you submit your solutions, you should respond to the corresponding post on Piazza with an overview of your analysis and the main results. In that post, you should also reflect on your analysis, commenting on matters including, but not limited to, limitations and potential extensions. If there are parts of your analysis about which you are unsure, you can raise those questions in your Piazza post! You will be required to respond to feedback and suggestions made by your fellow students about your solution. You must also provide constructive feedback to your peers’ solutions to exercises you have not submitted.

You should upload your Quarto markdown file (i.e., a .qmd) at this submission link. If you use external data (i.e., data that cannot be directly loaded into R), then you should submit a compressed archive (i.e., a .zip or a .tar.gz file) that contains the data and your Quarto markdown file. Be sure to include your name and the exercise number (e.g., 01-1 for Lecture 01, Exercise 1) in your submission’s filename.

Lecture 01

01-1: Best Shooting Performances in the WNBA

The package wehoop provides functions for scraping play-by-play and box score data for women’s college and professional basketball. Using that package, replicate the analysis from lecture to identify the best shooting performances in the WNBA between 2022 and now. To load in box score data, you will need to use the functions wehoop::load_wnba_player_box() and wehoop::most_recent_wnba_season() to load the raw box score data.

ImportantDifferences between the NBA and WNBA

The NBA and WNBA have different All-Star dates and the WNBA dataset may have slightly different column names than the NBA dataset. So, you will need to modify some parts of the code from Lecture 1 to run this analysis!

01-2: Four Factors

Dean Oliver, pioneer of basketball analytics, introduced Four Factors to describe how teams win. The Four Factors are computed from box score data at the team level and attempt to summarize a team’s performance in terms of shooting, rebounding, turnovers, and free throws. Using data from either the NBA or WNBA, compute the four factors for each team in every season since 2002. Which teams’ were the best in terms of each of these four factors?

01-3: Offensive Efficiency

Dean Oliver introduced an offensive efficiency metrics that tracks how many points are produced by an individual player over the course of 100 possessions. While it’s possible to compute offensive efficiency exactly using play-by-play or player-tracking data, these data were not always publicly available at the time that Oliver was writing. Oliver developed a formula for approximating offensive efficiency using only information contained in the box score; the offensive rating page on Basketball Reference has the full derivation.

Using either NBA or WNBA, identify the player-seasons with the highest and lowest offensive ratings.

ImportantIntermediate Variables

To compute offensive rating formula, you will need to compute several intermediate variables that summarize both team and individual performance. Performing this analysis will require some clever grouping operations!

Lecture 2

02-1: Over- and Under-performing XG

For each team in EURO 2022, sum their \(\textrm{XG}_{i}\) values and sum the residuals \(Y_{i} - \textrm{XG}_{i}.\) Which teams most over- or under-performed expectations? What relationship, if any, do you observe between teams’ cumulative XG and their performance relative to the StatsBomb XG model’s expectations?

02-2: EURO 2025 Performance

During Lecture 2, we worked with data from EURO 2022 tournament. Hudl has released similar data from the EURO 2025 tournament (which was also won by the Three Lionesses). Repeat the analyses from Lecture 2 to identify the EURO 2025 players with the most extreme goals over expected values in that tournament. Similarly, repeat the exercise in Section 3.1 to identify teams that most over- or under-performed expectations (in terms of goal scoring) during EURO 2025.

02-3: League Performance

Hudl’s free data includes full seasons from several top professional leagues. Using the data from all complete league seasons explore the relationships between (i) the cumulative \(\textrm{XG}\) the team generates on offense; (ii) the cumulative \(\textrm{XG}\) the team gives up on defense; and (iii) their overall win-loss record. Do these relationships vary across leagues?

Lecture 3

03-1: \(\textrm{XG}\) and the Shot Cone

For every shot attempt, we can form a triangular region by connecting the shot location to the goal posts (whose coordinates are (120, 36) and (120, 44)) and connecting the two goal posts. We will refer to this region as the “shot cone”. Add two new feature to the data frame wi_shots that we created in Lecture 3: ConeAngle, which measures the angle between the lines connecting the shot location to the goal posts and ConeArea, which is the area of the shot cone.

Intuitively, what sorts of relationships would you expect between ConeAngle and ConeArea and XG?

ImportantNote

You do not necessarily need to fit any model to answer this question!

03-2: Predictive Utility of ConeAngle and ConeArea I

Using 100 random training/testing splits, how much predictive accuracy is gained (if any) by including ConeAngle, ConeArea, and both ConeAngle and ConeArea as additional features in the random forests model we built in Lecture 3?

03-3: Estimating XG Using More Data

In Lecture 3, we trained our XG models using only data from women’s international competitions. Train an XG model using data from at least 5 different full competitions contained in the public StatsBomb data. How similar are the XG predictions of your new model to the proprietary XG values provided by StatsBomb?

Lecture 4

04-1: Sensitivity of (Weighted) APM

In Lecture 4, we set a 250 minute threshold and collapsed all players below that threshold into a single baseline player. Re-fit the APM and weighted APM models using different choices of minutes cut-offs (e.g., 100 or 300). How much do the top- and bottom-10 player rankings change?

04-2: Re-thinking Stint Weights

In Lecture 4(lectures/lecture04.qmd#sec-wapm}, we weighted stints using only the lead at the start of the stint. One could reasonably argue that weights should also account for the time left in the game. Propose your own weighting scheme and see how the player rankings change.

04-3: Predictive Quality of APM Models

Estimate the out-of-sample predictive mean square error of the basic APM model using 100 training/testing splits built in Lecture 4. That is, for each training and testing split, fit each model using the training data and then compute the average of the squared difference between the actual \(Y\)’s and the model predictions in the testing split. Is the basic APM model a good predictive model? Why or why not?

Lecture 11

11-1: Replicate RSAR Analysis

Replicate the full Runs Saved Above Replacement analysis for the 2023 and 2024 regular seasons. To what extend do catchers’ RSAR values vary season-to-season? What are potential implications of such variability to roster building?

11-2: Pitcher Framing Metric

We used the catcher random effects from a multilevel model to estimate how much more each catcher contributed to the probability of a called strike than a replacement-level catcher. Those estimated contributions formed the basis of the catcher metric RSAR.

One could argue, however, that pitchers are responsible for at least some of the value created by pitch framing; after all, for a catcher to frame a pitch successfully, the pitcher must throw a frameable pitch! Using the multilevel model from Lecture 11, create and compute an analog of RSAR for pitchers.

11-3: Adjusting for Batter and Pitcher Quality

The historical GAM model we built in Lecture 11 only accounted for pitch location. That model and the multilevel model we fit to estimate catcher effects did not adjust for the quality of the batter or pitcher. It is at least conceivable that umpire’s may call the strike zone for an elite, veteran batter differently than they would for a batter who has just been called up. Re-fit the historical GAM to adjust for the batter’s wOBA and pitcher’s ERA, both computed at the end of the 2024 regular season, in addition to the pitch location. Similarly, re-fit the multilevel model to adjust for batter’s wOBA and pitcher’s ERA computed at the end of the 2025 as fixed effects. How do the resulting RSAR estimates and catcher rankings change after we adjust for batter and pitcher quality in both component models.
