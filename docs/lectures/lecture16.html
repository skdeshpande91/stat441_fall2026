Lecture 16: NFL Drive Simulation I

Motivation: NFL Overtime Rules

ImportantAcknowledgement

This analysis extends a fantastic case study first published by Mike Lopez.

National Football League (NFL) games consist of four 15-minute quarters. Prior to 1974, when the score was tied at the end of the fourth quarter, the game was declared a tie. Between 1974 and 2010, the league adopted a “sudden death” overtime period: both teams would play for an additional 15 minutes and the first team to score any points during that period was declared the winner. Under the sudden death rules, there was a clear advantage to possessing the ball first: by kicking a successful field goal, you could win the game without giving the opposing team an opportunity to possess the ball. As kickers steadily improved, the winner of sudden death overtime became increasingly determined by the result of the coin flip used to determine first possession.

In 2010, the NFL adopted “modified sudden death” overtime rules in which both teams have a chance to possess the ball unless the first team scores a touchdown on their opening possession. If the second team gets possession (e.g., by forcing a turnover, a punt, or giving up only a field goal to the first team), the winner is whichever team has the higher score at the end of the second team’s possession. If the game is still tied after the second team’s possession, the team that scores next wins the game.

In 2019, the Kansas City Chiefs proposed a further change to the NFL’s overtime rule to allow both teams an opportunity to possess the ball, even if the first team scored an opening drive touchdown1. Since both teams are guaranteed possession, there is arguably some advantage to deferring possession: the second team will know exactly how many points they need to score in order to win or extend the game. To quantify the potential advantages and disadvantages of receiving the ball second under the proposed rules, the NFL League Office set out to estimate the probability that the second team, when trailing by a touchdown, actually scores a touchdown. If this probability is extremely low, there is not much justification for the rule change.

Overview

Over the next several lectures, we will use play-by-play data from 20 NFL seasons (1999-2000 to 2018-19) to

  • Establish the advantage of possessing the ball first under sudden death and modified sudden death overtimes
  • Estimate the distribution of outcomes for the second team’s possession after giving up an opening drive touchdown under the proposed rules

At first glance, both of these tasks seeem straightforward. The advantage of first possession under the sudden death overtime rules can be quantified using the probability that the first team kicks a field goal or scores a touchdown on their opening possession. To compute this probability, we can look at all games played between 1999 and 2009 and count the number of times the team with first possession scored on their opening overtime drive. To assess the potential benefits of possessing the ball second under the proposed rules, it is tempting to find all drives immediately following an opening drive touchdown and compute the proportion of those drives that also resulted in a touchdown. While there are many such drives, none of them quite capture the unique scenario presented by the proposed overtime rules. That is because, under the proposed rules2, if the first team scored an opening drive touchdown, the second team

  1. Will never punt or kick a field goal on fourth down3
  2. Must score a touchdown to extend the game
  3. Are relatively unconcerned about the clock4

We will instead evaluate the proposed rules by simulating entire drives for the team possessing the ball second in overtime while trailing by a touchdown. Rather than building a statistical model to predict the number of yards gained on any one play given the down, distance, and field position (hereafter starting context), we will instead use re-sampling. Specifically, we will simulate the outcome of the current play by randomly selecting a historical play with similar starting context and using its outcome.

Play-by-Play Data

We will work with NFL play-by-play data that has already been scraped by tne nflfastR team. These data are available online and can be loaded into an R session using the nflfastR::load_pbp() function.

raw_pbp <- nflfastR::load_pbp(seasons = 1999:2018)
save(raw_pbp, file = "nfl_pbp_1999_2018.RData")

The data frame raw_pbp contains 937379 rows, each recording the values of 372 different variables. Each row corresponds to a different entry in a play-by-play table (like this one from a 2016 game between the Cowboys and Packers). The nflfastR team provides a searchable data dictionary that describes each varible. Of particular importance for our purposes are the variables fixed_drive and fixed_drive_results, which were constructed by nflfastR and, respectively, index drives within games and record the result of each drive.

Benefits of First Possession in Overtime

As noted in Section 1, there is an arguably an intuitive advantage to possessing the ball first under the sudden death overtime rules: if the first team scores any points at all, they immediately win the game without giving the other team an opportunity to possess the ball. To quantify this advantage, we will count the number of overtime games played between 1999 and 2009 in which (i) the first team ultimately wins the game and (ii) the first team wins the game as a result of an opening drive score.

To these ends, we must first identify all regular season games between the 1999 and 2010 that went to overtime. The following code filters the full play-by-play data table to include just those plays from overtime. Then, it extracts a unique game identifier (the variable game_id).

Setting Up For Drive Simulation

Under the proposed overtime rules, both teams would have an opportunity to possess the ball even if the first team scored an opening-drive touchdown. At least on paper, these rules give the second team an opportunity to win or extend the game, regardless of how the first team’s possession ends. Further, this guaranteed possession may change the decision calculus for teams when it comes to electing to receive the ball first. Specifically, one could argue that the proposed rules favor the second team as they would know exactly what they’d need to do to win or extend the game.

As noted in Section 1, we will build a drive simulator to estimate the probability that the second team, when trailing by a touchdown in overtime, is able to extend the game by scoring a touchdown. Our simulation will work by repeatedly sampling the outcome of each simulated play from the pool of actual plays with similar starting context, which we define as the combination of position on the field (yardline_100)9, down (down), and distance to the first down marker (ydstogo). In the simulation, we use the starting context and sampled outcome of each play to determine the starting context of the next simulated play.

To power our simulation, we need to build a pool of representative plays from which to sample outcomes based on the following criteria:

  1. The possessing team should be trailing by 6, 7, or 8 points: we’re interested in estimating how likely the second team is to scoring a touchdown after giving up an opening drive touchdown in overtime
  2. The play must come from a drive that does not span multiple quarters: under the proposed overtime rules, the second team loses if their drive doesn’t end before the end of the overtime period
  3. We should exclude all plays from punt or field goal formation: under the proposed overtime rules, the second team will never attempt a punt or field goal

We will further restrict our pool of plays to be those from regular season games between the 2008 and 2019.

pbp <- 
  raw_pbp |> 
  dplyr::filter(season %in% 2008:2018 & season_type == "REG")

To identify drives spanning multiple quarters, we first group raw_pbp by both game_id and fixed_drive. Then, for each combination of game and drive identifier, we can count the number of distinct values of qtr that appear in the rows of the play-by-play sub-table. If the drive spanes multiple quarters, this count will exceed 1.

multi_qtr_drives <- 
  pbp |>
  dplyr::group_by(game_id, fixed_drive) |> 
  dplyr::summarise(n_qtrs = dplyr::n_distinct(qtr), .groups = "drop") |>
  dplyr::filter(n_qtrs > 1)

Determining Starting and Ending Contexts

Each row in pbp contains several variables recording the context (i.e., yard line, down, distance to the first down marker, quarter, time remaining, scores) at the start of each play-by-play entry. In our simulation study, every time we randomly sample a representative play (based on the starting context), we will determine the ending context for that play and use that to set the starting context for the next simulated play. Unfortunately, nflfastR does not automatically record the ending context. So, we will need to manually add columns to our dataset recording the ending context.

To this end, we will

  1. Remove all plays from drives spanning multiple quarters using the function `dplyr::anti_join()
  2. Group the remaining plays by both game_id, game_half, and fixed_drive10 3, Arrange the rows by the number of seconds remaining in the game
  3. Look up the next value of several contextual variables using the function dplyr::lead().
raw_plays <-
  pbp |>
  dplyr::anti_join(y = multi_qtr_drives, by = c("game_id", "fixed_drive")) |>
  dplyr::filter(season_type == "REG" & game_half %in% c("Half1", "Half2")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(game_id, game_half, fixed_drive) |>
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(game_seconds_remaining)) |>
  dplyr::mutate(
    next_yardline_100 = dplyr::lead(yardline_100),
    next_down = dplyr::lead(down),
    next_ydstogo = dplyr::lead(ydstogo),
    next_half_seconds_remaining = dplyr::lead(half_seconds_remaining)) |>
  dplyr::ungroup() |>
  dplyr::arrange(game_id, dplyr::desc(game_seconds_remaining))

Now, each row of raw_plays contains the starting and ending context for each play. So, in our simulation code, as soon as we sample a play based on its starting context, we can immediately determine the starting context for the next play using the newly constructed variables next_yardline_100, next_down, next_ydstogo, and next_half_seconds. However, because we only need to simulate the second possession (and not subsequent possessions), we need to stop the simulation if a sampled play ends with a touchdown or turnover. One way to do this is to set the ending context variables to NA for such plays and then include some conditional logic in our simulation code to draw the next play only when the ending context variables are not NA.

Unfortunately, as shown during lecture, the code block above does not correctly set next_yardline immediately after touchdowns. In the code block below, we manually set the ending contextual variables to NA following a touchdown, interception, or turnover.

raw_plays <-
  pbp |>
  dplyr::anti_join(y = multi_qtr_drives, by = c("game_id", "fixed_drive")) |>
  dplyr::filter(season_type == "REG" & game_half %in% c("Half1", "Half2")) |>
  dplyr::group_by(game_id, game_half, fixed_drive) |>
  dplyr::arrange(dplyr::desc(game_seconds_remaining)) |>
  dplyr::mutate(

    next_down = dplyr::lead(down),
    next_ydstogo = dplyr::lead(ydstogo)) |>

    next_yardline_100 = dplyr::case_when(
      touchdown == 1 ~ 0,
      fumble_lost == 1 | interception == 1 ~ NA,

  dplyr::ungroup() |>

We’re now in a position to build our pool of representative plays. In doing so, we need to restrict attention to those drives where the possessing team trails by 6, 7, or 8 points. Because the second team will never line up in a field goal or punt formation under the proposed overtime rules, we will also remove fake field goal and punts.

Starting Position

Our simulation will work by randomly selecting the outcome of actual plays with similar starting context as the current simulated play. We have so far built a pool of representative plays from which to sample the outcomes conditionally given the starting context. The very last required ingredients are randomly sampling the starting field position and the amount of time remaining for the second possession.

To determine the distribution of potential starting positions and the amount of time remaining in the overtime period, we can first identify all regular season games featuring a touchdown in the first drive. Then, we can get the starting context for the first play of the second possession in each of those games Because the proposed overtime period is only 10 minutes long, we need to exclude those games in which the first team scored an opening drive touchdown in more than 10 minutes.

opening_score_games <-
  pbp |>
  dplyr::filter(fixed_drive == 1 & fixed_drive_result == "Touchdown") |>
  dplyr::pull(game_id) |>
  unique()

drive2 <-
  pbp |>
  dplyr::filter(game_id %in% opening_score_games & fixed_drive == 2) |>
  dplyr::filter(score_differential %in% c(-6, -7, -8)) |>
  dplyr::arrange(game_id, dplyr::desc(game_seconds_remaining))

drive2_start <-
  drive2 |>
  dplyr::group_by(game_id) |>
  dplyr::filter(play_type %in% c("run", "pass", "no_play") & kickoff_attempt == 0) |>
  dplyr::slice_head(n=1) |>
  dplyr::ungroup() |>
  dplyr::select(yardline_100, quarter_seconds_remaining) |>
  dplyr::filter(quarter_seconds_remaining > 300)
1
Regulation quarters are 15 minutes long. So, if at least 300 seconds remain in the quarter at the start of the second possession, the first drive took less than 10 minutes.
